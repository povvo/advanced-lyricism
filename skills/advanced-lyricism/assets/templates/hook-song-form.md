## Quick operating card

> Use for execution. For rationale, edge cases, cross-domain reasoning and tool interpretation, read the paired deep reference.

A hook is a stable identity point while verses create change. The skill of song form is managing **return, difference, information, and contrast**.

## Hook function
Stability can come from:
- melody;
- rhythm;
- wording;
- image;
- harmony;
- question/command;
- one rhyme family;
- a production gap;
- a repeated social address.

Decide what the listener should recognise before deciding how many words to repeat.

## Archetypes
- **melodic** — pitch/vowel contour carries identity;
- **chant** — rhythmic/social participation carries identity;
- **phrase** — one verbal unit is the anchor;
- **hybrid** — two or more of the above.

Choose by beat, voice, and dramatic need.

## Compression
Hooks often benefit from:
- fewer simultaneous ideas than verse;
- shorter syntax;
- memorable stressed vowels;
- one central image/proposition;
- clean participation points;
- breath/production space.

Do not write a dense verse and rename it chorus.

## Repetition legitimises—until it stops developing
A repeated phrase can become meaningful through familiarity. But repetition should have a reason:
- identity;
- ritual;
- obsession;
- proof of a loop;
- participation;
- changed context.

If return adds nothing, shorten or alter it.

## Repetition-with-difference
Develop a hook by:
- changing one word while keeping cadence;
- keeping words while verse context changes their meaning;
- answering from another viewpoint;
- statement <-> question;
- changing pronoun/addressee;
- removing or adding a final line;
- changing final stress/landing;
- returning the same image after consequence.

Use **sequence + recurrence + variation** as a meaning engine.

## The gap
Negative space can be part of hook identity:
- missing expected word;
- production gap;
- response slot;
- held vowel;
- stopped phrase;
- beat of silence.

The listener should be able to anticipate enough to participate. Strategic silence can be as memorable as another line.

## Drop / return placement
A hook can feel larger because of what happens **before** it:
- density narrows;
- phrase length shortens;
- rhyme complexity drops;
- one unresolved word is held back;
- drums/bass clear space;
- final verse line points toward the hook’s first vowel or rhythm.

The “drop” is relational. If everything is already maximised, there is nowhere to arrive.

## Verse-hook contrast
Contrast can use:
- density;
- line length;
- pitch;
- entry point;
- rhyme complexity;
- specificity;
- pronouns;
- register;
- emotional distance;
- camera scale;
- amount of silence.

Keep one bridge variable so the song still feels like one system.

## Signposts and endpoint
For long-form planning, know a few **signposts** and a destination even if the route stays flexible.

For a song this may mean:
- opening image;
- first irreversible turn;
- hook 1 meaning;
- verse 2 complication;
- final hook meaning;
- last image.

Do not over-outline every bar.

## Reveal architecture
Information placement can change the form:
- reveal before hook -> hook becomes dread/irony;
- reveal after hook -> next return reclassifies it;
- hide one fact until bridge/last verse -> retrospective payoff;
- disclose to listener before speaker -> dramatic irony.

A repeated hook is especially powerful when the words stay stable but listener knowledge changes.
